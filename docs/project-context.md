## 1. The final project

The overall idea is this. In your group,

* Pick an area that you are excited about
* Find a dataset about that domain (much more on this later)
* Use the tools you've learned in the class to explore the dataset and find something new and exciting about your dataset that nobody knew.
* Deliver three things: a short scientific paper (PNAS template, LaTeX only, at most six pages including references) that we will evaluate. And a short in-person presentation (three slides, three minutes) in the final session, where we can ask questions. Plus a half-page reflection on how the course's "From an AI-drafted report" examples worked for you, ungraded.

The full description, and how to pick a dataset and a question, is in week 8.

### Grading

Test 1 (25%), Test 2 (25%), and the final project (50%) will be scored, and I'll provide each score as feedback so you can see how you're doing. For the final evaluation, I map the three scores holistically to a final grade on the Danish 7-point scale.

The scores. For the two tests you simply get your raw score. The project gets one score on a four-step scale: 0 insufficient, 1 sufficient (the minimum), 2 good, 3 excellent. That single number covers the paper and the presentation together: at least two of us (the TAs and I) read every paper and sit in on every presentation, each of us scores it 0–3 in a handful of categories (listed in week 8: the research question, data and scope, use of the course, checks, figures, references, the presentation, overall impression), we discuss each one, and we agree on the number. The scores are feedback, not grades, and there is no simple formula from them to the 7-point scale; the final grade is a holistic evaluation of your work across the whole course. If someone sits right at a grading boundary, the go-nuts posts count too, as a tie-breaker. If you want more than the number, ask: we are happy to explain why you got the score you got and what would have made it higher.

## 2 · The exam: what, how, why

The final project is the exam. It counts for 50% of your grade, it runs over weeks 9–13 on a dataset you pick, and here is what I am asking for.

The formalia in one box: the project checklist on the front page.

### What you deliver.

* **A scientific paper.** Written in the PNAS template, in LaTeX only (the Overleaf template; no Word version this year), at most six pages including references. The sections of a real paper: Abstract, Significance Statement, Introduction, Results, Discussion, Methods, References, plus author contributions (Section 7 walks through them). Uploaded as a PDF to DTU Learn by Friday 27 November, 23:59, so we can read every paper before we see you.
* **A short presentation.** Three slides, three minutes, in the final session, Wednesday 2 December, in front of me, Lasse and the TAs, followed by questions. The slides are a three-page PDF that you hand in together with the paper; we stitch everyone's into one deck and leaf through it on the day, so nobody plugs anything in. The point of the presentation is mainly to check if you know your stuff, so you'll pitch the project and we'll ask technical questions mainly to check if you understand what you've handed in.
* **A half-page reflection.** One PDF page at most, handed in with the paper, not graded. Two questions: did the "From an AI-drafted report" boxes on the week pages change how you read your own draft, and where in your own report work did you use them (or not)? Honest answers help us teach the course better next year; "they did not help" is a fine answer.

### How it is graded.

The project gets a single score on the course's 0–3 scale, paper and presentation together. Each of us who reads your paper scores it 0–3 in each of the categories below, with a comment, and after the presentations we meet and agree on the one number you get. The categories, so you know what we look for:

* **The research question.** Is there one, is it interesting, and is it specific enough to be answered?
* **Data and scope.** The dataset contains both networks and text, it fits the question, and it took some ambition to get: collected, combined, or looked at in a way nobody has.
* **Showcasing the course.** You used the network science and the NLP from the course to learn something from your data, and you understand the methods you applied.
* **Analysis and checks.** The measurements are checked: a null, a second method, or a hand-labeled sample, with the result reported, and you say what your measurements actually measure.
* **Figures.** Are the figures awesome and serve the story? Also the basic stuff, like labeled axes, captions that say what to see.
* **References and literature.** Are you able to connect your work to the world around you and the scientific literature? References should be real, relevant, correctly cited, and should place the project in context: what was known before you started, what you added.
* **Overall impression.** One story, told well: a strong abstract and introduction, readable text, a sense of hard work and thoroughness.
* **Presentation and questions.** Three slides that pitch the project in three minutes, and a group that holds up under questioning.

### Why it looks like this.

Look at your group's go-nuts posts. Seven weeks, seven analyses, each one more than I could have asked of a student group a couple of years ago. You have proven you can create near infinite analyses - and I hope you have realized how much awesomeness you are capable of putting together. But the project and the exam deliberately do not reward more of that.

The exam format is chosen to grade two things:

1. **Can you pick an interesting problem?** And
2. **Can you communicate it clearly?**

A plain template with no room to spare means you lose a lot of the advantage that comes from the agents: flashy designs, volume, volume, volume. You cannot pile up endless nice-looking analyses, because they do not fit in the template, and a reader who sees six pages of everything can easily tell that you didn't focus.

The paper is about the one thing agents still cannot fully do for you: deciding which question is worth six pages. Just like a real scientific paper, a six-page paper is a great format for a single question, the analyses that support it, and reflections about how your methods impacted your findings. You have to carefully choose the two or three figures that carry the story.

The presentation part is about testing whether you know your stuff. If your agent wrote the pipeline, each of you still has to be able to say what it does, why the numbers are right, etc. We will ask whoever we like, so everyone in the group must be able to explain everything in the paper.

We kick off with a guest. Piotr Sapiezynski is an assistant professor at DTU Compute and an assistant research professor at Northeastern University. He audits online platforms and their algorithms, working with other researchers, journalists and NGOs, and his results have e.g. fed into a $5 billion FTC settlement with Facebook. In this class you will learn how to find undocumented APIs in the wild, how to automate collecting data through them, and how they've been used by researchers and investigative journalists.

## 5 · The coolest dataset you can find

Here is why Piotr opens the day.

Every group in this room can download a Wikipedia category. Every group can find the same ten "network datasets" with the same ten Google searches, and I have read the papers that come out of them. The single biggest advantage a project can have is a dataset with an insight worth digging out of it, and after this morning you will learn about ways of finding insanely interesting data that most people do not know exists.

Think about what you looked at on your phone this week. The streaming service that recommends shows, the app that lists concerts in Copenhagen, the second-hand marketplace, the running app with its routes and kudos, the game with its match history, the recipe site including reviews, the real-estate listings, the sports site with every lineup since 1990. Each one of those pages is filled by a request you can probably see, copy, strip down and loop. Behind many of these apps you'll find a dataset with people in it, sometimes connections between them, and text they wrote, and few of them have been studied. Perfect for this class. Piotr's case studies are the proof: a YouTube ad-keyword blocklist found by watching which searches returned an empty response (how they did it), Amazon's own brands listed by a filter nobody documented, and a million internet-service plans collected in two days (the method). Those were investigations. Yours is a project, but the strategy is the same: find the request, and the dataset is yours.

### Many other sources of data.

Ambitious scraping of undocumented APIs is not the only way to go. There are many conventional datasets online that have carried great projects, and a familiar source with a fresh question beats a rare source with none. Some places to start:

* **Wikipedia**, any category or list, with the crawl from week 1; the whole encyclopedia is one API. And Wikipedia back in time: every page's revision history is in the same API, so you can rebuild a category's network and text as it looked in 2010 and ask how it changed.
* **The wikis** devoted to shows, games, bands and books on Fandom and elsewhere, which run the same software and the same API as Wikipedia: Wookieepedia, the Game of Thrones wiki, the Simpsons wiki, the Marvel and DC wikis, Bruce Base for every Springsteen show, and one for almost anything with fans.
* **arXiv**: every paper's title, abstract, authors and categories (bulk metadata, or the API); co-authorship is the network, abstracts are the text, and OpenAlex (week 4, exercise 4.4) or Semantic Scholar add the citations.
* **Bluesky**, which has an open, documented API and a firehose: follows and reposts are the network, posts are the text, and it is young enough that most questions about it are unasked.
* **Reddit**, through the official API or the archives: a subreddit is a community, the users and their replies are the network, the comments are the text. Hacker News has the same shape and a simple public API.
* **IMDb** (the datasets) and **MovieLens**: actors, films and crews as a network, plots and reviews as text.
* **Music**: Genius for lyrics, MusicBrainz for who played with whom, Last.fm and Spotify's playlists for who is listened to together.
* **Parliaments**: the Danish Folketing publishes every speech, member and vote as an open API; the UK Hansard and the US Congressional Record likewise.
* **Books**: Project Gutenberg has tens of thousands of novels; the characters in one, or across an author's work, are a network you build with week 7's NER.
* **GitHub**: repositories, contributors, issues and README files, all through one documented API.
* **Catalogs**: Jim Bagrow's list, Stanford's SNAP and the Netzschleuder network catalog (mostly networks without text, so bring the text from elsewhere), and last year's list.
* **More in the Goodies.**

### Combine datasets.

This used to be a complicated/an impossible aspect: multiple sources, multiple formats, multiple sets of names that never quite match. With an agent, the joining is usually more like an afternoon. Lyrics plus collaboration credits, a character wiki plus the episode transcripts, a parliament's speeches plus its voting records, a subreddit plus the Wikipedia pages its users link to, the concert listings plus the setlists plus the artists' pages. The richest projects I have seen fused two datasets that nobody had put together, and the fusion itself was the first finding. Only the name resolution is still yours to check (recall week 7, Section 5): remember that two sources that both mention Stark have to agree on which one.

### The dataset questions.

Before you fall in love with a question, make sure the data can carry it. A dataset is worth your five weeks if:

* **It has both halves.** Nodes with links and text on the nodes, or text you can build a network from. The course is networks × language; the project should be too.
* **It is big enough to have structure and small enough to work with.** A few hundred to a few thousand nodes is the sweet spot. Below that, communities and distributions are noise. Above 10 thousand nodes everything becomes time consuming and your project will likely be more about managing compute than good questions.
* **You can get it without too much effort.** A documented API, an undocumented one, a dump, or a crawl you already have a pretty good idea of how to run. If getting the data is the project, you do not have a project. I've seen many projects die trying to crawl sites that didn't want to be crawled — e.g. Twitter or LinkedIn.
* **You already care about the domain.** You will read hundreds of these documents. Pick something where reading them is a pleasure, because you will also be the only person in the room who can tell whether a result is plausible.
* **Something about it is unknown.** Not unknown to science, unknown to you and to the people who care about that domain. "Which philosophers bridge traditions?" is a real question. "What is the degree distribution?" is a figure.

## 6 · Asking the right question

Now for tips about asking a good research question.

The question comes before the method. "We ran Louvain and word2vec on the corpus" is not a question. "Do the characters who are structurally central in the link network also sit at the center of the story, in the text?" is one. Notice that the second version tells you which methods to use. The first tells you nothing about why.

But it's complicated. Because sometimes the best questions arrive because you see that a method makes it possible to ask a new and interesting question. So don't be blind to the fact that methods can inspire good questions!

And also know that the VERY best questions arise from playing with the data. From iteration. In my own research I usually

1. Start from a question (that I think is brilliant).
2. Do some analyses and realize it was actually a pretty stupid question to be asking.
3. Ask a new and better question based on the results of the initial analyses.
4. Repeat that a few times until I have the right final question
5. Then I pretend that I was asking that final question all along.

And you are allowed to do that too. You are not bound by your initial idea. Allow it to develop as you work with data. Agents make those iterations cheaper and faster. Just remember that you need to have a clean and beautiful story when you're done. Then you put that story in the paper ;)

### Say what your analyses actually measure.

Modularity measures how grouped a partition is, not how right. A sentiment score measures what a lexicon or a classifier does on your text, not how a character feels. Cosine similarity between embeddings measures shared context in the training data, not shared meaning in your domain. Every analysis in your paper needs one sentence that says what it captures and one that says what it misses.

### Validate your choices.

Compare against a null (week 2), against another method (week 4), against a hand-labeled sample (week 7). If a model produced the number, you check the model on examples you read yourself, and you report the agreement. A result without a check is a claim about your software, not about the world.

### More on building a story.

This point expands on what I ended on when I was writing about iterating to the right question above.

When you write your project, build one single story, not a pile of techniques. Every figure should answer part of your question ... or set it up. If a figure does neither, you probably don't need it. If you get your story perfect, a reader can say what you found in a single sentence after reading your abstract (and every section after that should make the sentence more believable).

### Know what makes an analysis convincing.

It is not the method (sorry about repeating this so many times, but it's because it's a mistake that so many students make), it is the combination: a clear question, a measurement that fits it, a check that the measurement works, a comparison that rules out the boring explanation, and a limitation you found yourself before the reader did. A thing that I've written many times across the weekly lectures: your entire pipeline going from raw data to big complicated models and measures impacts your answer. Be aware of that in your writing and when you interpret and explain your results.

## 7 · Writing the report

I'm basically asking you to write a scientific paper. That's why I chose this format. The format is PNAS's own: start from the PNAS template on Overleaf on day one, and write into it, so you always know how much room you have left. Before writing anything, watch this.

The anatomy of a paper, section by section, and what each section is for. Watch it before you start writing, and again when you think you are done.

### The sections, and what each one is for:

* **Abstract.** The whole paper in 150 words: the question, the data, the main result with a number in it, and what it means. Write it last, and make it the sentence you want the reader to repeat.
* **Significance statement.** Two or three sentences for a reader outside the field: why should anyone who does not care about your domain care about this?
* **Introduction.** The question, why it is interesting, what is already known, and the one-sentence preview of the answer. Cite the work you build on; if a method or an analysis is someone else's, say so.
* **Results.** The story, carried by the figures. Each figure answers one part of the question, has a caption that can be read on its own, and shows the check next to the claim (the null, the baseline, the agreement with your hand labels).
* **Discussion.** What the result means, what it does not mean, and the limitation you found yourself. A negative result with a convincing check is a fine discussion.
* **Methods.** Enough that a reader could redo it: the data (what, from where, collected when, how many), every preprocessing choice that could change a number, each measurement and what it measures, each validation and its result.
* **References.** Academic standards; cite what you used.

### Figures carry the paper.

Three good figures beat eight. A figure of a network with a thousand nodes is a decoration; a figure that shows your effect against its null is a result. Every caption says what is plotted, what the comparison is, and what the reader should see.

### Author contributions must be specific.

"All group members contributed equally" is not a contribution statement. Who collected the data, who built which analysis, who did the hand labeling, who wrote which section.

## Essentials

There are no new terms this week. The Essentials are the judgment calls, written down so you can carry them into the project; none of them is on Test 2. Every one of them refers back to a method you already own.

* **The final project in total**: a six-page scientific paper in the PNAS LaTeX template (references included, link above), three slides and an ungraded half-page reflection, handed in on Learn by Friday 27 November; a three-minute presentation with questions on 2 December; 50% of the grade, one 0–3 score for paper and presentation together, on the choice of problem, the checks, the clarity of the story, and how it holds up under questioning.
* **The question comes before the method**: a project statement names something you want to know, and the method is whatever answers it. "We ran X" is a method; "does A predict B, and where does it fail?" is a question.
* **The best questions come from iteration**: start from a question, run the analysis, discover it was the wrong question, ask a better one, and repeat until the question and the data fit. Then write the paper as if you had asked the final question all along; you are allowed to, as long as the story you end with is clean.
* **What your analysis measures**: every measurement captures something specific and misses something specific: modularity scores how grouped, not how right; a sentiment score is a lexicon's or a classifier's behavior on your text; embedding similarity is shared context in training data. One sentence each, in the paper.
* **Validate**: check a measurement against a null (shuffle), a second method (NMI, agreement), or a hand-labeled sample (precision, κ). If a model produced the number, you report how well the model did on examples you read.
* **One story**: every figure answers the question or sets it up. The finding fits in one sentence, and the sections make the sentence believable.
* **Convincing**: a clear question, a measurement that fits it, a check that it works, a comparison that rules out the boring explanation, and a limitation you found yourself.
* **The dataset test**: both halves present (links and text); a few hundred to a few thousand nodes; obtainable in a day; a domain you care about; something unknown about it.
* **Text as attribute · text as network**: two ways to join the halves. A text measurement placed on nodes is an attribute, tested with a shuffle; entities extracted from text and connected by co-occurrence are a second network, tested by reading a sample of its edges.
* **Undocumented API**: the request a web page makes to fill itself with data, visible in the browser's Network tab; found, copied, stripped to its essentials, and looped from Python, it is a dataset builder. Not on the test.
